The Ship Called "Later" — Understanding Neduneer: The Quiet Habit of Postponement

  • 23rd September 2026

"Neduneer maravi madi thuyil naangum
Keduneerar kaamak kalan."

— Thirukkural 605

In the previous article, we stood metaphorically at a harbour.

Four ships were waiting there.

One whispered:
"Later…"

Another said:
"I'll remember…"

A third said:
"I don't feel like it now…"

And the fourth gently suggested:
"Just a little longer…"

Thiruvalluvar brings these four tendencies together and describes them through the powerful image of a "kalam" — a ship.

Now let us step closer to the first ship.

Its name is:
"Later…"

Thiruvalluvar calls this tendency Neduneer — நடுநீர்.

Arun Finds an Opportunity

Arun is a capable young man with many aspirations.

He wants to learn new things, find meaningful work and make something worthwhile of his life.

One day, he comes across an excellent training programme.

It seems almost perfectly suited to him.

Applications close in ten days.

A recommendation from a teacher is also required.

Arun is excited.

He thinks:
"This is really good. I must definitely apply."

There is no confusion about whether he wants the opportunity.

He does.

There is no external obstacle stopping him.

He only needs to begin.

He could message his teacher now.

Instead, another thought appears:
"There are still ten days. I'll ask the teacher tomorrow."

Nothing dramatic happens.

No opportunity disappears that instant.

No alarm sounds.

Arun simply continues with his day.

But something small has happened.

He has boarded the first ship.

Neduneer has begun.

What Is Neduneer?

Neduneer is unnecessarily stretching or postponing something that ought to be attended to without such delay.

It is important to understand this carefully.

Not every delay is procrastination.

Sometimes waiting is wise.

We may need more information before deciding.

Circumstances may not yet be suitable.

Another responsibility may genuinely deserve priority.

Sometimes the right action is precisely to wait.

The problem begins when there is no meaningful reason to delay, yet we repeatedly move an important action into an undefined future.

We know what needs to be done.

We may even genuinely want to do it.

But we tell ourselves:
"Later."

The Strange Power of "Someday"

Notice how often this appears in ordinary life.

"I'll start exercising next week."

"I'll meditate regularly from next month."

"I'll become serious about my studies next semester."

"I should spend some peaceful time talking to my mother. I'll do it someday."

"I'll start saving when my income improves."

"I want to learn that skill someday."

"One day, I'll start working on my dream."

There is nothing wrong with tomorrow, next week or someday.

The danger lies elsewhere.

When tomorrow arrives, we can always create another tomorrow.

"Someday" has no deadline.

And therefore, "someday" can quietly become "never" without our ever consciously deciding to give something up.

Some opportunities are not lost because we said:
"I don't want it."

They are lost because we kept saying:
"Not now."

When Procrastination Looks Like Productivity

Neduneer has another subtle form.

It does not always look like laziness.

Sometimes it looks remarkably productive.

Imagine someone who wants to write a book.

He researches the best writing software.

He watches videos about writing.

He buys a beautiful notebook.

He listens to interviews with successful authors.

He reorganises his desk.

He searches for the perfect writing routine.

He may be genuinely busy for several days.

From the outside, it appears that he is working seriously on his book.

But one question reveals the truth:

Has he written anything?

Perhaps not even one paragraph.

Preparation is valuable.

Research is valuable.

Planning is valuable.

But when preparation continually replaces the action it was supposed to prepare us for, something has changed.

Procrastination can disguise itself as preparation.

This is why simply asking:
"Am I busy?"

is not enough.

A better question may be:

"Am I doing the action that actually matters?"

Why Do We Say "Later"?

Sometimes we postpone because the task feels large.

Sometimes because we are uncertain.

Sometimes because beginning creates discomfort.

Sometimes because we want to do it perfectly.

Sometimes because there is something easier and more pleasant available right now.

And sometimes we may not even know why we are postponing.

That is why self-reflection matters more than self-criticism.

Instead of immediately telling ourselves:
"I am lazy."

we can become curious:
"What happens inside me at the moment I decide to postpone?"

That question helps us see the process rather than condemn the person.

Three Questions for Self-Reflection

This series is not merely about understanding Thiruvalluvar's words.

It is about allowing those words to become a mirror.

So pause for a moment.

Do not answer these questions about your child, spouse, student, friend or colleague.

Answer them about yourself.

1. What is one important thing that I know I should do, but keep postponing?

Do not make a long list.

Find one.

Perhaps it concerns your studies.

Health.

Work.

A relationship.

A difficult conversation.

A financial responsibility.

A skill you want to develop.

A dream you keep postponing.

What is it?

2. What reason do I repeatedly give myself for postponing it?

"I don't have time."

"I'm too busy now."

"I need to prepare properly first."

"I'll start when things settle down."

"I need to be in the right mood."

"This is not the right time."

What is the sentence you repeatedly tell yourself?

3. Is that reason genuinely necessary — or has my hesitation to act turned itself into a reason?

Do not rush to answer.

Sometimes the reason is completely valid.

If so, acknowledge it.

But sometimes what looks like a reason may actually be our reluctance to begin.

The purpose of the question is not to accuse ourselves.

It is simply to see clearly.

The Antidote Is Not "Finish Everything"

When we recognise procrastination, we often react by creating another impossible demand:

"I must finish the whole thing today!"

That may make the task feel even heavier.

There is a simpler response to Neduneer:

Begin.

Not:
Finish everything.

Just:
Take the next small meaningful step.

If you need to apply for something, open the application.

If you need a recommendation, send the message.

If you want to exercise, put on your shoes.

If you want to write, write the first few lines.

If you want to meditate, sit quietly for five minutes.

If you need to make a difficult call, find the number and make the call.

The entire journey does not have to happen today.

But the first step can.

"Later" Does Not Always Remain "Later"

Now let us return to Arun.

He told himself:
"I'll message the teacher tomorrow."

The next day became busy.

Two days later, while travelling, the application suddenly came back to his mind.

"Oh! I need to message the teacher for the recommendation."

This time, too, he did not reject the task.

He genuinely intended to do it.

He thought:
"I'll send the message as soon as I get home."

Then he reached home.

Something else demanded his attention.

A phone call came.

He ate.

He looked at his mobile for a while.

The day moved on.

And somewhere between intention and action... the task disappeared from his mind.

Yesterday, the voice had said:
"Later."

Today, something else had happened:
"I forgot."

And here we begin to see that these four ships may not always travel separately.

Sometimes one quietly leads us toward another.

"I'll remember…" — Maravi

Is human memory really a safe place to store everything we intend to do?
