Which Ship Are You Travelling In? A Journey of Self-Reflection Through a Thirukkural

  • 16th September 2026

“Neduneer maravi madi thuyil naangum
Keduneerar kaamak kalan.”

— Thirukkural 605

When does a person's life begin to move in an unwanted direction?

Is it only when we make a major mistake?
When we deliberately reject an important opportunity?
When we take one disastrous decision?

Not necessarily.

Sometimes, the direction of a life begins to change very quietly.

Four Familiar Voices

Listen for a moment to these four voices:

  • “I’ll do it later…”
  • “I’ll remember…”
  • “I don’t feel like doing it now…”
  • “Just a little more sleep…”

Do they sound familiar?

Perhaps we have heard them from our children, students, friends or colleagues.

But before looking at others, there is a more important question:

Have we heard these voices within ourselves?

None of them sounds particularly dangerous.

“I’ll do it later” can sound reasonable.
“I’ll remember” can sound confident.
“I don’t feel like it now” can sound harmless.
“Just a little more sleep” can even feel comforting.

Yet Thiruvalluvar brings four such tendencies together in one remarkable couplet:

நடுநீர் மறவி மடிதுயில் நான்கும்
கெடுநீரார் காமக் கலன்.

He speaks of:

  • நடுநீர் — Neduneer: unnecessarily delaying what needs to be done.
  • மறவி — Maravi: forgetting what needs to be remembered and acted upon.
  • மடி — Madi: inertia or unwillingness to make the necessary effort.
  • துயில் — Thuyil: excessive sleep.

But what makes the Kural especially striking is not merely that it identifies these four tendencies.

It is the image Thiruvalluvar chooses for them.

A Ship

He calls them a “kalam” — a vessel, a ship.

Why a ship?

Think about it.

Once we board a ship, we do not have to keep walking for the journey to continue.

We may sit comfortably.
We may talk.
We may look elsewhere.
We may even fall asleep.

Yet the ship continues to move.

And whether we are paying attention or not, it is carrying us in a particular direction.

Could some of our everyday habits work in the same way?

Could we be travelling even when we feel that we are simply “doing nothing”?

Could repeated postponement, forgetfulness, inertia or excessive sleep quietly carry our lives somewhere?

That is the inquiry at the heart of this series.

The Small Choices We Barely Notice

We usually pay great attention to life's big decisions:

  • Which course should I study?
  • Which career should I choose?
  • Whom should I marry?
  • Where should I live?
  • What should I do with my life?

These decisions certainly matter.

But between those big decisions are hundreds of smaller choices.

Do I begin now or postpone?
Do I record something important or trust that I will remember?
Do I take the necessary small step or wait until I “feel like it”?
Do I rest enough to participate fully in life—or allow comfort to consume the time meant for living?

One such choice may appear insignificant.

One day of postponement does not determine a life.
One forgotten task does not define us.
One moment of laziness does not destroy our potential.
One extra morning of sleep does not ruin our future.

But what happens when the same choice is repeated?

Again.
And again.
And again.

At some point, a repeated choice may become a pattern.

And a pattern may begin to carry us.

Like a ship.

This Is Not About Blaming Ourselves

The purpose of this exploration is not to label ourselves:

  • “I am lazy.”
  • “I am forgetful.”
  • “I have no discipline.”

Nor is this series intended as advice for parents to correct their children.

It is not a lesson for adults to give teenagers.

It is a mirror for all of us.

A teenager may postpone studying.
An adult may postpone attending to health.
A student may forget an assignment.
A parent may repeatedly forget a promise to spend time with a child.
A young person may lose hours scrolling.
An older person may keep postponing a long-desired change.

The form may differ.

The inner movement can be surprisingly similar.

So throughout this journey, instead of asking:

“Who else does this?”

we will keep returning to:

“How does this operate in me?”

That question changes everything.

Thirteen Questions — Not a Quiz, but a Mirror

As we explore these four tendencies over the coming articles, we will encounter 13 questions for self-reflection.

They are not examination questions.

There are no correct answers.

No one needs to see what you write.

They are simply opportunities to stop for a moment and look within.

For example:

  • What important thing do I know I should do, but keep postponing?
  • Where does my time actually go when I say, “I don't have time”?
  • Which of these four tendencies influences me most?
  • If I continue travelling in the same way for another five years, where might I arrive?

And perhaps the most practical question of all:

What is one small but meaningful action I can take today—not tomorrow—to change that direction?

Watching or reading something can give us information.

But transformation does not necessarily happen because we have understood an idea intellectually.

Sometimes it begins when an idea becomes a question—and we allow that question to look back at us.

So as you travel through this series, do not merely read.

Pause.
Observe.
Answer honestly.
And notice what becomes visible.

Four Weeks. Four Ships.

In the next four explorations, we will look closely at four familiar inner voices.

“I’ll do it later…”
What really happens when “later” becomes our habitual response?

“I’ll remember…”
Can good intentions survive if we entrust everything to memory?

“I don’t feel like doing it now…”
When does genuine rest end and avoidance of necessary effort begin?

“Just a little more sleep…”
Does our rest support our life—or slowly begin to take the place of it?

We will not explore them merely as abstract moral faults.

We will see how they appear in studies, work, health, relationships, dreams, digital habits and ordinary daily life.

And through the story of a young man named Arun, we will see how four apparently harmless tendencies can quietly come together around a single opportunity.

Before We Begin the Journey

Imagine standing at a harbour.

Four ships are waiting.

One whispers:
“Later…”

Another says:
“I’ll remember…”

A third says:
“Not now…”

And the fourth gently invites:
“Just a little longer…”

None looks frightening.

That may be precisely why we board them so easily.

Thiruvalluvar's Kural invites us to do something before travelling further:

Look at the ship.

Not with fear.
Not with guilt.
With awareness.

Because before asking how fast we are travelling, perhaps we should first ask:

Which ship am I travelling in?
