Upekkhā: The Art of Remaining Balanced Without Losing Love

  • 5th August 2026

A Continuation from the Previous Article

In the previous article, we explored Mettā, the ninth of the Ten Pāramīs. Mettā taught us a beautiful truth: A heart that sincerely wishes for the well-being of all beings is Mettā. Through Ravi's story, we discovered that one of the greatest acts of kindness is simply choosing not to increase another person's suffering. Mettā asked us an important question: "Does my presence make another person's life even a little better?" But does life always unfold the way we hope? Will everyone we love understand us? Will everyone we help change? Will every sincere effort bring the results we expect? The answer is no. Life is full of change. Sometimes there is success. Sometimes there is failure. Sometimes there is praise. Sometimes there is criticism. Sometimes there is gain. Sometimes there is loss. Amid all these changing conditions, can our mind remain peaceful and balanced? This question leads us to the tenth and final Pāramī: Upekkhā.

Meena's Story

Meena was a school teacher. For many years she had lovingly taught her students. There was one boy who worried her deeply. He was intelligent. But he showed little interest in his studies. He often got into fights. He rarely completed his homework. Meena spoke with him many times. She encouraged him. She met his parents. For months she did everything she could. Yet very little seemed to change. One evening after school, Meena sat quietly in her classroom. She felt tired. A question arose within her: "I am trying so hard. Why is nothing changing?" Just then, an elderly teacher came and sat beside her. He gently said, "You can plant the seed. You can water it. But you cannot decide when it will sprout." Those words stayed with Meena. From the very next day, she did not reduce her love. She did not stop caring. She did not give up on the boy. Only one thing changed. She slowly let go of her need to control every outcome. She continued to be the best teacher she could be. But she stopped carrying every result on her shoulders. Several years later, the same student returned to visit her. He smiled and said, "If you had given up on me then, I would not be standing here today." At that moment Meena understood something deeply. Love is necessary. Effort is necessary. But inner balance is equally necessary. This is Upekkhā.

What Is Upekkhā?

Many people misunderstand Upekkhā. They think it means, "Not caring." That is not what the Buddha meant. Upekkhā is not indifference. It is not emotional coldness. It is not becoming detached from people. Upekkhā is: The ability to remain inwardly balanced without losing love. We continue to care. We continue to love. We continue to help. But we do not allow life's changing circumstances to throw our mind into constant disturbance. That is Upekkhā.

The Lesson of the Balance Scale

Imagine a balance scale. If one side becomes heavier, the scale naturally seeks balance. Only then can it measure accurately. Our mind is very similar. Too much attachment disturbs it. Complete indifference also disturbs it. Upekkhā is the balanced middle. It neither clings nor withdraws. It remains steady.

Why Is Upekkhā Important?

We care about people. We work hard for them. We support them. Yet they may misunderstand us. Sometimes our sincere efforts fail. At such moments, two dangers appear. One is to say, "I will never help anyone again." The other is to think, "Everything depends on me. I must control every outcome." Upekkhā protects us from both extremes. It gently reminds us: "Give your best with all your heart. But do not try to control every result."

The Deeper Perspective of the Abhidhamma

The Abhidhamma describes a beautiful mental quality called Tatramajjhattatā. It means mental balance or remaining centred in the midst of experience. Whether pleasure arises, or pain, whether praise comes, or blame, whether success arrives, or failure, the mind remains balanced. This balance does not come from suppressing emotions. It comes from understanding them with wisdom. Paññā gave us clarity. Viriya gave us effort. Khanti gave us patience. Saccā gave us truthfulness. Adhiṭṭhāna gave us determination. Mettā gave us unconditional goodwill. Upekkhā now holds all these qualities together in perfect balance.

How Can We Recognize Inner Balance?

We can ask ourselves a few simple questions. When someone praises me, what happens inside me? When someone criticizes me, what happens? When success comes, do I lose perspective? When failure comes, do I fall apart? Our answers reveal the stability of our mind. As Upekkhā develops, the world around us may remain exactly the same. But the way we respond to it begins to change.

How Upekkhā Transforms Us

Upekkhā gradually transforms us into calmer human beings. Impulsive reactions become fewer. We are no longer carried away by every emotion. Our need to control other people slowly fades. Acceptance grows. Clarity deepens. Most importantly, Upekkhā teaches us how to love deeply without losing inner peace.

Conclusion

Dāna taught us to share. Sīla taught us to live rightly. Nekkhamma taught us to let go of unhealthy attachment. Paññā taught us to see clearly. Viriya taught us to persevere. Khanti taught us patience. Saccā taught us to live truthfully. Adhiṭṭhāna taught us to remain committed to what truly matters. Mettā taught us to cultivate goodwill toward all beings. Now Upekkhā teaches us the final lesson. Let there be love. Let there be care. Let there be wholehearted effort. But let there also be inner balance. Because, Upekkhā is not the absence of love. It is the wisdom that allows love to remain balanced. This is the culmination of the Ten Pāramīs.
